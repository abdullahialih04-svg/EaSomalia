Career Guide

Your career is your
biggest lever for good.

Around 80,000 hours — that's roughly how long you'll spend working across a lifetime. It's the largest resource most people ever control, and it's usually allocated with far less deliberation than a house purchase.

The research organisation 80,000 Hours argues that which problem you work on matters more than which employer you join — because the scale and neglectedness of a problem can differ by orders of magnitude, while the difference between two good employers rarely does.

In Somalia's context, that means asking a harder question than "what job can I get?" Where is skilled attention genuinely scarce? Which problems are large but under-worked? Where does one well-placed person change what happens to many?

These aren't questions with clean answers, and anyone who gives you one should be treated with suspicion. But they're answerable well enough to beat choosing by default.

Framework

Importance, neglectedness, tractability

A rough tool, widely used across effective altruism, for comparing problems that otherwise resist comparison. It won't give you a ranking you should trust blindly — but it will tell you which questions you haven't asked.

I

Importance

How many are affected, and how severely?

Scale, depth, and duration. A problem that harms many people badly and for a long time scores higher than one that inconveniences many briefly.

N

Neglectedness

How much attention is it already getting?

The less crowded a problem, the more one additional person tends to add. This is where small communities often find their advantage.

T

Tractability

How much progress would more resources buy?

Some enormous problems resist effort almost entirely. Others yield steadily. Importance without tractability is a way to waste a career sincerely.

Where we focus

Where we think the leverage is

Three areas we point members toward, plus the Somali-specific questions that sit alongside them. We're not claiming these are the only things worth doing — they're where we think the case is strongest and where we can actually help you.

01 · Global health

The strongest evidence base there is

Decades of trials mean we can say with unusual confidence what malaria prevention or nutrition programmes actually achieve per dollar. For clinicians, epidemiologists, health economists and data people, the work is well-defined and the impact is measurable.

Much of it happens in contexts close to our own, which makes Somali professionals unusually useful rather than incidental to it.

02 · AI safety

Large, and thinly staffed

AI systems are being deployed faster than anyone has worked out how to make them reliable, and the number of people working on that gap is small relative to its scale — a textbook case of neglectedness.

It's the hardest of the three to enter from Somalia, and we won't pretend otherwise. But it's a field young enough that entering it now means entering it early.

03 · Animal welfare

Among the most neglected areas in philanthropy

An enormous amount of suffering receives a very small share of global charitable attention, which means marginal funding and effort go further here than almost anywhere.

It's also a live question in an economy where livestock is central — one most people here have practical knowledge of already.

Somali context · Giving

Somalis already give. The question is where it lands.

Generosity isn't the missing ingredient here. Between zakat, sadaqah, and support sent to family and community, Somalis give a great deal — often a serious share of their income, and often without being asked.

What's harder is knowing which organisations turn that money into the most good. GiveWell's research finds large differences in cost-effectiveness between charities working on the same problem — which means the choice of where to give can matter as much as the decision to give at all. That's a question worth a few hours of anyone's attention, whatever their career.

EA Somalia recommends effective giving through Giving What We Can and GiveWell, which direct donations to carefully evaluated, high-impact charities. Our giving guide also explains how to approach zakat and sadaqah in ways that align with effective giving principles.

Somali context · Food security & digital access

Two problems where the response quality varies enormously

Recurring drought puts large numbers of people at risk, and the responses available differ hugely in how well-studied and how cheap they are per life improved. That variation is where analytical work earns its keep.

Digital access is the other. A generation coming online at once is a generation making decisions — about work, money, and what to believe — with tools it hasn't been taught to use. Building that capacity is unglamorous and compounding.

Pathways

Four routes worth considering

Broad archetypes rather than job titles. Most real careers mix them, and most people move between them more than once.

Research

Generate the evidence that guides where money and attention go. Somalia is under-studied, which means well-designed local research can influence decisions far larger than itself.

Health researchPolicy analysisEconomics
Suits people who can tolerate slow feedback and care more about being right than being fast.

Policy & advocacy

Shape the rules that determine how public resources are allocated. The leverage here is real but indirect — you rarely see your own effect, and attributing outcomes is genuinely hard.

Government advisoryNGO policyLegal advocacy
High ceiling, high variance. Depends heavily on being in the right room at the right moment.

Building things

Start or join organisations that solve a problem directly and can grow. In Somalia this covers fintech, agricultural technology, and health delivery — areas where the gap between what exists and what's possible is wide.

FintechAgri-techHealth delivery
Most attempts fail. That's a reason to expect failure, not a reason to avoid trying.

Earning to give

Take a high-earning career and fund work that others are better placed to do. For diaspora professionals earning in hard currency, the exchange rate alone makes this substantial.
